ABSTRACT

Unfilled restaurant reservations cause substantial revenue loss and leave dining capacity idle. RestroVibes tackles this by providing a platform where clients and restaurateurs coordinate table bookings through a transparent, data-driven process. Every reservation is checked against live seat and table capacity before it is committed, so the same slot can never be sold twice. Each client is assigned a reliability score computed from their past behaviour, and whenever several guests compete for one contested slot, a weighted lottery awards it fairly to the most flexible, loyal, and dependable candidate. Background jobs keep the system self-managed by auto-confirming quiet bookings, detecting overstays, and recording no-shows, while GPS-based search helps clients find nearby restaurants along with live occupancy and travel estimates.

The platform is implemented as a React single-page application talking to a Node.js and Express API backed by PostgreSQL through the Sequelize ORM. Communication is protected with a JSON Web Token access and refresh scheme over RESTful endpoints. The outcome is a responsive and modular product that centralises appointments, table capacity, reliability scoring, and customer relations in one coherent system.

Keywords: Restaurant Booking, React, Node.js, Express, PostgreSQL, Sequelize, REST API, JWT, Weighted Lottery, Reliability Scoring

Chapter 1: Introduction

1.1 Introduction

Dining out is one of the most personal and location-dependent services a business can offer, yet the technology behind booking a table has lagged far behind. Most restaurants still rely on phone calls, paper ledgers, or generic calendar tools that give neither the guest nor the owner a reliable picture of what is actually available. RestroVibes is a restaurant appointment booking platform built to close that gap, offering a single place where clients can find a nearby restaurant, check live availability, reserve a table, and a restaurateur can track every booking, enforce seat capacity, and assess the reliability of the people asking for tables.

The system supports three different kinds of users. Clients can search for restaurants and make reservations, while restaurateurs manage their services, tables, and seating capacity, and admins supervise the entire platform. Underneath this simple interaction model lies a careful set of rules that keep bookings honest: capacity is checked before any reservation is accepted; each client carries a reliability score that influences how their requests are treated; and contested slots are resolved through a weighted lottery rather than raw timing.

1.2 Problem Statement

The restaurant industry loses a meaningful share of its revenue to guests who never arrive. A no-show wastes a seat for an entire time window, and there is usually no chance to refill it. The reverse problem is equally disruptive: when several guests request the same slot, the restaurateur is forced to make a quick decision with almost no information about who is most likely to show up. Both problems spring from the same root cause — reservations are managed without live capacity data and without a consistent, fair policy for deciding between competing requests.

Existing tools that merely store bookings do not solve this, because they record intentions without checking feasibility. What is missing is a system that validates each booking against real capacity, learns from guests' past behaviour, and applies an objective rule whenever demand exceeds supply. RestroVibes was designed specifically to supply those missing pieces.

1.3 Objectives

The following objectives guided the design and development of the system:

  • to offer a unified place where clients and restaurateurs can manage reservations end to end;
  • to validate every booking against live seat and table capacity before it is committed;
  • to derive a reliability score for each client from their booking history;
  • to allocate contested slots fairly through a weighted lottery;
  • to automate routine decisions such as accepting quiet bookings, detecting overstays, and recording no-shows;
  • to help clients discover nearby restaurants together with live occupancy and travel time.

1.4 Scope and Limitation

This part of the report draws the boundary around what the system does and what it deliberately leaves out, so that expectations about the platform remain realistic.

1.4.1 Scope of System

RestroVibes covers the whole journey of a table reservation. Clients can register, locate restaurants on a map, and request bookings, while restaurateurs maintain services and tables, set their seating capacity, and confirm or reject reservations. Behind those screens, the platform computes reliability scores, runs the weighted lottery, and keeps the appointment lifecycle in order through background workers. It also supports a two-way rating system so that both sides of the table can give feedback about a completed visit.

1.4.2 Limitation of Existing System

While the platform covers a great deal of ground, a few boundaries were left in place:

  • distances are measured as straight-line, great-circle distances rather than road-based routes;
  • payments are recorded for reporting purposes but are not actually processed;
  • reliability scores depend on accurate and consistent booking history; and
  • a single organisational structure is supported, so linked branches of one restaurant are not yet handled together.

1.5 Development Methodology

The project followed an iterative, feedback-driven development process. Rather than attempting to build everything in one pass, the work moved through repeated cycles in which a small set of features was designed, coded, reviewed, and refined before the next cycle began. This Agile approach made it easy to adjust priorities as requirements became clearer and kept each module testable from an early stage. The technology choices — React for the interface, Node.js with Express for the backend, and PostgreSQL for storage — were selected because they are modular, well documented, and scalable enough to accommodate future growth.

Chapter 2: Background Study and Literature Review

2.1 Background Study

Reservation management sits at the centre of a restaurant's daily operations. Keeping tables full while honouring every confirmed guest requires accurate, up-to-the-minute knowledge of what is free, and that knowledge must be shared between the person taking the booking and the person serving the table. Where this coordination is handled manually, double bookings and forgotten reservations are common, and there is little historical record to learn from when guests fail to arrive.

Over time, restaurants have moved from paper registers toward software that stores reservations, but most of those tools stop at simple record keeping. RestroVibes goes further by linking each reservation to real capacity, by tracking the reliability of each guest, and by applying a clear policy when demand exceeds what a single slot can hold. These additions turn booking from a bookkeeping task into a management decision supported by evidence.

2.2 Literature Review

A large body of prior work informs the design of the platform. Web-based reservation services, used widely in the hospitality sector, demonstrate how centralised booking with live table availability and customer feedback improves the experience for both diners and restaurants [1]. That thinking is echoed in the appointment and rating modules of this system.

Research on fair allocation of limited resources highlights the value of assigning guests to slots according to a measurable score rather than simple arrival order [2]. RestroVibes adopts that principle in its weighted lottery, where flexibility, loyalty, and reliability each contribute to a weight that determines who wins a contended slot. Studies of reputation and scoring systems also confirm that visible, consistent scoring shapes behaviour over time [3], which supports the decision to expose a client's reliability status to restaurateurs.

Finally, work on automated service-lifecycle management shows that predictable, supervised automation — such as accepting a quiet booking or flagging an overstaying guest — removes routine burden from staff and keeps operations consistent [4]. The background workers in this system operationalise that idea, leaving the restaurateur free to focus on guests rather than bookkeeping.

Chapter 3: System Analysis and Design

3.1 System Analysis

Analysis establishes what the platform must accomplish and the conditions under which it must operate. The requirements were gathered by considering the everyday workflow of a small restaurant and the frustrations of both restaurant owners and their customers, then translated into concrete functions and quality expectations.

3.1.1 Requirement Analysis

Requirements fall naturally into two groups: what the system must do, and how well it must do it.

  1. Functional Requirements
  2. Non-Functional Requirements

i. Functional Requirements

The functional requirements describe the concrete actions the platform supports:

  • Management of Accounts – create, log into, and administer client, restaurateur, and admin accounts.
  • Reservation Handling – create, confirm, cancel, complete, and extend bookings.
  • Capacity Checks – verify seat and table availability before accepting a request.
  • Reliability Accounting – compute and maintain each client's reliability score.
  • Slot Dispute Resolution – resolve contested slots using a weighted lottery.
  • Map-Based Discovery – show nearby restaurants with occupancy and travel time.
  • Two-Way Feedback – allow clients and restaurateurs to rate one another.
  • Supervision and Evidence – provide dashboards, risk profiles, and demand summaries.

ii. Non-Functional Requirements

The non-functional requirements describe how the platform should behave:

  • Responsiveness – availability and bookings update without noticeable delay.
  • Growth – the design must tolerate a growing number of clients and restaurants.
  • Safety – endpoints are protected with token-based authentication.
  • Stability – graceful error handling and reliable data storage.
  • Ease of Use – a clean, responsive interface that requires little training.

3.1.2 Feasibility Study

i. Technical Feasibility

The chosen tools are mature and widely documented, which greatly reduces technical risk. React provides a responsive interface, Node.js and Express offer a lightweight and high-performance backend, and PostgreSQL supplies a robust relational store with support for transactions and enumerations. Everything is packaged with Docker so that the same configuration runs consistently on any machine.

ii. Operational Feasibility

The platform addresses real, day-to-day concerns of a restaurant: preventing double bookings, limiting no-shows, dealing fairly with competing requests, and keeping capacity visible at all times. Automating the routine parts of the booking lifecycle also lightens the load on staff, making the system easy to adopt in practice.

iii. Economic Feasibility

Because the entire stack is open source, development and licensing costs remain low. Deployment through containerisation keeps hosting predictable, and a modular codebase means that future changes can be made without rewriting large parts of the system.

iv. Schedule Feasibility

3.1.3 Object Modeling

The class diagram expresses the structural backbone of the system. The central entity, UsersModel, stores identity, role, location, service hours, and seating capacity; a single user may appear as client, restaurateur, or admin. Bookings are captured by AppointmentModel, which links a client and a restaurateur through a service and records date, party size, and status. A restaurateur owns RestaurateurService offerings and TableModel tables, while RatingModel and BookingHistoryModel preserve feedback and behavioural records. Contested slots are represented by LotteryPoolModel, which holds the weight assigned to each entry. Together these classes define how users, services, tables, ratings, history, and lottery entries relate to one another.

3.1.4 Dynamic Modeling

Dynamic modelling captures how the system behaves over time. The sequence diagram in Figure 3.4 walks through the two key workflows: making a reservation (including the lottery path) and the background handling of overstays.

Booking and Lottery Flow

  • A client sends a request for a restaurant, date, slot, and party size.
  • The system counts the existing pending requests for that slot.
  • If the slot is free, an appointment is created and accepted immediately.
  • If it is contested, a lottery entry is stored with a computed weight.
  • The lottery scheduler later selects a winner, accepting that booking and cancelling the rest.

Overstay Handling

  • The overstay worker looks for active bookings whose end time plus a grace period has passed.
  • It marks them completed, writes an overstay record, and refreshes the client's reliability score.

3.1.5 Process Modelling

The activity diagram illustrates the life of a single reservation from request to settlement. A request is checked to see whether the slot is contested; an uncontested booking may be auto-accepted after a grace period, while a contested one passes through the lottery. Once the time arrives, the arrival is recorded, delayed arrivals are penalised, and, if a booking outlives its window, the overstay worker completes it. Every transition feeds the scoring engine, which keeps each client's reliability status current.

3.2 System Design

Design converts the analysed requirements into a concrete structure for the software: how it is organised internally and how it is deployed.

3.2.1 Component Diagram

The component diagram shows the platform separated into clear layers. At the top, a React interface lets each actor reach the system. Below it, Express controllers accept HTTP requests and route them into the business-logic layer, where the scoring engine, the weighted lottery, and capacity management do the real work. The persistence layer, realised with Sequelize models, translates those operations into SQL against the PostgreSQL database. This separation keeps each part of the system easy to test and change independently.

3.2.2 Deployment Diagram

The deployment diagram describes a three-tier arrangement. The client tier hosts the built React application behind Nginx, which also forwards API calls upstream. The application tier runs the Express backend, the business logic, and the background workers, talking to the data tier over the PostgreSQL driver. The database tier holds all records — users, appointments, services, tables, ratings, history, and lottery entries — and is reached only through the application tier, keeping the data layer shielded from direct client access.

3.3 Algorithm Details

Two algorithms lie at the heart of the platform: reliability scoring and the weighted lottery. Their working mechanism is summarised in Figure 3.8.

Reliability Scoring

Each client's score begins with the behaviours observed on their booking history. A no-show, being late, cancelling late, or overstaying is assigned a weight reflecting its cost to the restaurant — a no-show is the most damaging, an overstay the least. Those weighted counts are combined and divided by the total number of bookings to obtain a raw penalty. A decay term, grown from the share of bookings that were completed, then softens the raw value so that consistent good behaviour reduces the penalty without erasing it entirely. The resulting score between zero and one is mapped to a reliability status: reliable, at risk, or flagged.

Weighted Lottery

When several clients want the same slot, each is given a weight starting from a base value. The weight increases with flexibility (the willingness to consider nearby times) and with loyalty (repeat business and account age), and decreases with the client's penalty score. An ageing factor is then added so that earlier entries carry a slight advantage. A weighted random selection then picks the winner, whose booking is accepted while the remaining requests are cancelled. This gives deserving, flexible, and reliable guests a fairer chance than a plain first-come-first-served rule would.

Location Discovery

Nearby-restaurant discovery relies on the Haversine formula to measure the great-circle distance between the client and each restaurant. Travel time is estimated at a modest average speed to keep the figure understandable. This means the "distance" shown is a straight-line measurement rather than a driving route, a deliberate simplification chosen for cost and simplicity.

Chapter 5: Conclusion and Future Recommendation

5.1 Conclusion

RestroVibes demonstrates that a booking platform can do more than store reservations; it can actively protect a restaurant's capacity and pool. By checking every request against live seat and table availability, the platform removes the possibility of overbooking at the source. By scoring each client's reliability, it gives the restaurateur a clear, honest picture of the risk each request carries. And by resolving contested slots through a weighted lottery, it turns an often arbitrary decision into a fair, explainable rule.

The use of React, Node.js, Express, and PostgreSQL produced a responsive and dependable product, and the background workers keep the appointment lifecycle in order with little human input. Altogether, the platform improves operational efficiency, gives both sides of the reservation clearer information, and lays a solid foundation for further growth.

5.2 Future Enhancement

Several extensions could build on the current system in later releases:

  • Actual payments – connect a payment gateway so deposits or cancellation fees can be charged automatically.
  • Road-based travel times – replace straight-line distances with real routing from a mapping service.
  • A mobile application – allow booking and management while on the move.
  • Automated alerts – send confirmations, reminders, and no-show notices by email or SMS.
  • Predictive analytics – forecast demand and flag likely cancellations before they happen.
  • Multi-branch support – let a restaurant manage several locations from one account.
